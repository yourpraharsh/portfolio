Gold: getting first-time shoppers to trust a higher price

Gold is Meesho’s premium product programme: better-rated products at a higher price. On the app, a Gold product looked like any other listing, with only a small Gold tag, so shoppers saw only the bigger number. I redesigned the journey from listing to order confirmation so Gold is noticed, understood and trusted, and Gold’s share of orders doubled in a one-month experiment.

What shoppers said

In our user research sessions, shoppers who’d never bought Gold didn’t know it existed, and didn’t trust a high price without proof.

Shoppers who hadn’t noticed or bought Gold, about 99%

“Gold ke bare mein to nhi suna kabhi.” I’ve never heard of Gold on Meesho.
“Saste mein same jaise lagne wale options mil rahe hain, toh wahi le leta hoon.” I can find similar-looking options for less, so I buy those.
“Pehle exchange/return mein dikkat aayi thi, ab dubara risk nahi lena chahta.” I had trouble with a return or exchange before. I don’t want to take that risk again.
“Agar reviews ya ratings premium quality nahi dikhate, toh bharosa nahi hota.” If the reviews and ratings don’t show premium quality, I don’t trust the premium tag.

Regular Gold buyers, under 1%

“Gold se kai baar saman mangwaya hai, toh ab pehchanne lag gaya hoon.” I’ve ordered Gold several times, so now I recognise it easily.
“Pasand nahi aaya toh wapas kar sakta hoon, ye achha laga.” I liked that I can send it back if I don’t like it.
“Main Meesho pe image search karke wo saman dhundta hoon jo dusre site pe mehenga mil raha tha.” I use image search on Meesho to find cheaper versions of things that cost more on other sites.

Two problems

Recall and comprehension

Gold didn’t register

Shoppers couldn’t recall what Gold stood for, and a Gold product looked and read like any normally priced one.

Decision to buy

It looked overpriced

The page gave the same details as a cheap product, so Gold read as the same item at a higher price. Shoppers could find similar-looking options for less, and past bad experiences with quality or returns made them wary of paying more.

What was already working

  • Returns lowered the stakes. Knowing they could send it back made shoppers more willing to try.
  • Repeat buyers recognised Gold and were happy with the quality.
  • Some shoppers came to Meesho on purpose to find cheaper versions of products they’d seen on premium sites.

What we took from it

The name stayed. The screens had to explain it.

Gold was the top pick overall, and in every shopper group we split by. But no name explained itself: only about one in six read Gold as what it actually is, high quality at a higher price, while a third or more read every name as offers. So we kept Gold and made the design say what it stands for.

Visual product details

Gold products were described with the same plain list of attributes as any cheap product. We explored showing the attributes that justify the price, like silk cloth, a sweetheart neckline or a rectangular blouse, as images, and how much of the page they should take.

Made with an image model, not by sellers

We generated these visuals with an image-generation model, giving it each product’s details and photos. Asking sellers to make them wasn’t practical for an experiment:

  1. Most of our sellers aren’t tech-savvy enough to create them.
  2. With the outcome unknown, they had little reason to put in the effort.
  3. Getting them made through operations would have been too much work for a test.

Show the reason for the price

Each banner names one attribute in Gold type, next to a crop of the product that shows it.

Side by side, always open

The final shows the attribute tiles side by side in one short row, open by default, with the full attribute list behind More Details.

The horizontally stacked tiles won for two reasons. They don’t depend on a shopper tapping to open them, and one short row doesn’t push down the product-page recommendations, which bring in most orders.

What went liveThe product page’s Gold theme went live, along with the listing and transition changes. The other product page changes never went live because other priorities took over.

What testing showed

Shoppers linked the new Gold tag to quality, and found the extra detail on the product page helpful. But they read seller reviews as reviews of the product.

The tag worked

On the listing, shoppers noticed the new tag placement and connected it with quality.

The detail registered

On the product page, they noticed there were more details than usual.

Seller trust got mixed up

Seller reviews and ratings were taken as the product’s own quality, which shaped how we labelled the seller section.

Setup: control against two test versions.

Next initiatives, after this cycle

  1. Bring reels onto the product page, so shoppers understand the value props even better.
  2. Replace the cash-on-delivery value prop with one that speaks to Gold: the plan is to introduce faster returns and exchanges.
  3. Check how seller rating and product rating relate, before leaning on seller trust.
  4. Find more ways to bring the right shoppers to Gold in the right context: the discovery problem, scoped out of this cycle at prioritisation.
Experiments

The listing changes went live first, and the version without video won.

Two A/B tests measured the awareness work on the listing: the Gold pill joined to the rating, and the value-prop animation. Apart from its Gold theme, the product page changes never went live because other priorities took over.

Joining the Gold pill to the rating on its own lifted Gold orders, and orders and gross order value (GMV) across the platform rose too, so Gold’s gain didn’t come out of other products.

With and without video on the listing

We then compared two versions of the listing: one with a video card, and one without. The version without video worked better. Without the video, the first card in the feed wasn’t held back, so shoppers converted better on it.

What the tests taught us

  1. The listing alone moved Gold. Joining the Gold pill to the rating raised clicks and orders on Gold products, before any product page change.
  2. Gold grew without taking from the rest. Platform orders and GMV per visitor rose slightly in every version.
  3. Motion has a cost on the feed. A video on the listing drew the eye away from the first card, and the version without it won.
Impact

In a one-month experiment, Gold went from 1% to 2% of all orders on Meesho.

The lift came from the design changes that went live: the listing, the transition, and the product page’s Gold theme. The rest of the product page changes never went live because other priorities took over.

Reflection

Three things I’d carry into the next premium launch.

Premium needs proof, not a label

Premium isn’t a label you add. It’s proof, at every point where a shopper might doubt the price.

Motion has a cost on the feed

A video on the listing drew the eye away from the first card, and the version without it won.

Name what each signal is about

Seller reviews were read as product reviews. Labelling the source of every trust signal clearly from the first draft would have saved a round.

The short version

When your size was missing, the same product often sat in another seller’s listing. We put it right under the size you picked.

What was built

Background and context

Many sellers list the same kurti. Discovery only ever showed size L’s price and availability.

On Meesho, many sellers list the same product, or near-identical designs, as separate listings, each with its own sizes, prices and stock.

The core problem

Every listing was priced and ranked on size L.

Of all the breaks, these two journeys cause shoppers the most pain in the live app.

Why not just swap in another seller’s listing?

When a size sold out, we could have quietly switched the shopper to another seller’s listing, or to the best seller for that size. We didn’t, because we wanted shoppers to make a conscious choice.

Swap quietly

Shoppers buy something they didn’t check

Swap visibly

Friction right after they’ve decided

Outside-in

Amazon, Myntra, AJIO and Flipkart keep sizes in order and strike out unavailable ones in place; Myntra adds stock counts and a size recommendation from past orders. None of them offered the same product in your size from another seller, which Meesho’s multi-seller catalogue makes possible.

When the size is out of stock

Where our journey dead-ended, and how these apps solve it

On Meesho, a missing size or a better price elsewhere sent shoppers back to browsing. The apps above catch that moment instead: AJIO and Temu send a sold-out size straight to other products in the shopper’s size. The fix brings the same idea onto the product page, right after a size is picked.

Edge cases

Size ranges up to 20XL, plus-size shoppers whose size is rarely stocked, duplicates surfaced under reviews before any size is picked, and pairing alternatives with the product you’re on.

Other categories size differently, and the same size row has to work for them too:

  • Women’s innerwear: sizes are band and cup (32B, 34C). Only the exact size fits, so an option one size away is no help.
  • Baby and kids wear: sizes go by age (0–3 months, 2–3 years). Parents shop for a child’s age, so options must match it exactly.
  • Footwear: sizes are numbers (UK 6, 7, 8). The size chart differs from brand to brand, so the same number can fit differently.

Why no tags

After all that, we dropped the tags.

Tags carried much of this work: the case rules above, the icons, and the copy we tested. In the end, none of them shipped.

We laid all of this complexity out to product, business and design leaders as a group, and asked them one question: should we show tags at all? Seeing the full picture, the group decided to drop them, for four reasons.

“Best” and “better” are subjective

The best-priced option could be poor on quality, and cases like that came up a lot.

“Best” stopped being possible, or needed

Best quality first meant the best-rated option for this product. When the definition changed to 5-star rated, there would rarely be a “best”, only “better” options, under a single icon, so the tags couldn’t tell options apart. And many times only one duplicate was shown, so a tag had nothing to set it apart from anyway.

Shoppers read numbers, not labels

In user research, people looked at price and rating first and the tag after. Reading English is hard for many of them anyway.

A tag needs a rank

Unless the tagged product came first in the rail, its tag would get lost, so there was no point. But ranking on one axis wasn’t right and would have cost conversions.

So the final rail shows price and rating side by side and leaves the choice to the shopper.

Two guiding principles, straight from V1 and V2

  1. Shoppers must tell the three payment modes apart at a glance, so POP doesn’t cannibalise prepaid.
  2. Keep it simple and clean. Build understanding without overloading the payment page with numbers and visuals.

A flywheel, not a permanent option

POP is a starter, not a place to stay. Once a shopper has paid with POP one to three times, depending on how likely they are to pay online, it switches off for them.

  • By then, trust is built. They’ve seen part-paid orders arrive, which is enough trust in Meesho to pay online in full.
  • Full payment is the natural next step. Paying online in full carries the bigger discount, so the move happens on its own.
  • Keeping POP longer adds noise. For a shopper ready to pay in full, an extra option only gets in the way.

The V3 setup: a 10% advance with 25% of the prepaid discount. One earlier reason for POP, the balance problem, was now handled by Pay Before Delivery (PBD, paying online after placing the order), which had scaled.

About Hevo

A no-code way to move data from one place to another

My product design internship project at Hevo Data ran for about two months. I found UX issues, validated them with quantitative and qualitative data, and redesigned the interface to fix a particular metric, working with the design team, a product analyst, multiple PMs, the CTO, and the sales and solutions teams, especially to set up user research, which did not exist before.

Hevo Data is a B2B SaaS tool that helps data teams build ETL and reverse-ETL pipelines: in basic terms, move data from one point (say Shopify) to another (say Google BigQuery) without code, with one source and one destination per pipeline. Users create multiple pipelines under their plan; pricing is per data point moved, with a 14-day free trial.

Some SaaS sources skip the sign-in and go straight to authentication details.

The problem

Pipeline growth was negative for most SaaS sources

My role

Find UX issues in the pipeline product, validate them with data and user research, and fix them after feedback from the design team and multiple PMs.

The metric

The product team tracks the % growth in pipelines made from each data source over two months. For most SaaS-source pipelines it was negative.

Why it mattered

Hevo’s whole revenue model is the data points moved, which only grows when the number of pipelines grows. Negative growth was bad for revenue.

Spotting the UX problem

Users were stuck at the source connection step

I started by watching Fullstory session recordings of users trying to make a pipeline with a SaaS source. The authentication page is different for each source, and in many sessions users could not complete connecting the source; most of them were not interacting with the required fields at all.

Observations from user sessions

Six patterns, and what each one meant

Users clicked the active Test CTA repeatedly while required fields were empty and an error showed up

Inference
  • The CTA was active even when required fields were not filled
  • The error message didn’t guide users on how to solve the error

Users filled non-compulsory fields but not the required ones

Inference
  • It was not clear enough which fields were required
  • There is too much cognitive load

Users readily engaged with fields that had options to select from

Inference
  • It was easier to select from options than to type

Users read a search-and-fill field as a scroll, find and fill field

Inference
  • The affordance of the field as a search field was weaker than it should have been

The product has a guide, but it wasn’t much help and few users used it

Inference
  • The guide didn’t have the information users actually needed at that moment

Searching for tables or data from some sources needed opening a particular folder to select the file inside

Inference
  • The search experience for finding files was broken
  • An option was only selected when the checkbox was tapped; the whole row wasn’t clickable
User research

The first 1:1 interviews and usability sessions the team had run

Some things were impossible to learn without talking to users: why they failed at sign-in, when that page opened in a separate tab; what stopped them from filling in the authentication page, beyond what Fullstory showed, such as trouble finding the information needed; and why drop-off differed between sources with similar authentication pages.

Planning

I proposed 1:1 interviews plus usability sessions, wrote a research plan, and presented it to the design team, PMs and CTO. It was approved, though it hadn’t been done before.

Who

Trial users (under 14 days old): they were new to pipeline creation, and the people dropping off at sign-in were mostly new users, since existing users had saved accounts. They could also tell us first-hand how hard it is to learn to connect a source.

How

Sourcing B2B users for 1:1s was almost impossible without the sales team. So I joined calls where the sales and solutions team helped trial users with pipeline difficulties. Once their issue was solved, I asked them to connect a source while sharing their screen, observed, and asked leading questions afterwards.

Outcomes

  • Users were not sure how to select tables to make a report for their custom needs, so they just picked Standard Report
  • Users thought Load historical data would bring a charge, but it was free. Since it was compulsory with no way to turn it off, they were scared of being charged
Competitive analysis

What Fivetran, Hightouch and Stitch do at the same step

Fivetran

  • Shows the available list rather than blank fields
  • Makes optional fields clear, as against compulsory and non-editable ones
  • Shows the steps ahead instead of non-essential content in the guide on landing
  • Uses strong visuals, like pictures of the source product and arrows, to guide users
  • Shows what is being tested and exactly where a failure happened

Hightouch

  • Clear communication about the coming steps
  • A preview of the incoming data from the source
  • Freedom to choose which step to complete first

Stitch

  • The destination connection has good guiding information: it provides what a field needs just before the field, in a scannable, point-wise way
Solution

Reduce drop-offs on the authentication page, to drive SaaS pipeline numbers

After the user research, the Fullstory insights and the competitive analysis, I came up with many ideas, then discussed them with the PMs of different sources to confirm what was technically feasible and worth investing in. These were agreed to be taken up:

Field states

  • Error, success, optional and compulsory, editable later or not
  • The CTA stays inactive until all required fields are filled

Guide redesign

A guide that helps users while they are making a pipeline, not a generic description of the source

Group similar fields

Segregate related fields together to reduce cognitive load

Errors that point

The error message guides the user to the field causing it and says explicitly what went wrong

Multi-select that looks searchable

Increase the affordance of the object-selection field so it reads as searchable, and make selecting options usable

Chips for static fields

Non-dynamic fields with few options, such as region, become chips, reducing anxiety

Saved credentials

Credentials entered earlier for sources without a sign-in step are saved and shown automatically, cutting time and effort

Historical data, clarified

Clarity over the historical data field, with a switch for it

Selection, done from the backend

  • The whole row is clickable, not only the checkbox
  • Global search across accounts, files and sheets
Visuals

The redesign, screen by screen

Guide redesign

  • The introduction contributed to clutter
  • All of the content was visible even when no user needed it
  • Collapsible sections inside each tab, so users control what they see and what they don’t

Responsive text field iterations

  • Colour only on the field causing the error does not communicate what happened with the other fields
  • Too much distraction with colours on all fields; not sure where to focus, causing cognitive overload
  • With a tick icon it is easier to scan which fields to ignore, since they are correct, and where to focus
  • Helps error prevention: it only lets the user click once all required fields are filled

Checking the connection

  • The CTA activates when all the required fields are filled
  • On Test, a status block shows the real-time status of each step being tested
  • The block turns red if there is an error, tells which part caused it, what the error is, and gives a CTA to edit that field
  • Until the error field is edited, the CTA stays disabled
  • The user is taken back to that field with the property highlighted, to ease finding the error
  • The user comes back down to Test
  • The CTA changes state to active
  • If all the parts pass, the block turns green with the appropriate message

Increasing the affordance of search and fill

  • Changed the design to increase the affordance of the field as search-and-fill
  • Copy nudges the user to type keywords to start searching
  • As soon as a keyword is typed, the search starts and results show
  • Stays searchable when filled, with the ability to remove entered values
  • When more than one option is selected, each is visible and can be removed separately
  • If the full width of the field is used up, the height expands

Chips, and dark mode

  • Chips replace non-changing, limited-option fields, to ease and accelerate users
  • Hevo has both light and dark mode; I made designs for all changes in both

Historical data import

  • Historical data import is now switch-based, with chips to select the period from
  • It can be turned off as well, since it is going to be a paid service soon

Retain credentials

  • SaaS sources that have no sign-in step, and only need details on the authentication page, now retain the credentials entered before

Grouping and hiding

  • Similar fields are grouped together to reduce cognitive load
  • Inactive fields hide, so users focus on the current fields, which helps reduce errors

Impact: by the time I left the company these changes had not been implemented, so I could not see what they moved.

Challenges and learnings

What was hard, what could have been better, and what stayed with me

Challenges

  • Bringing all stakeholders together for user research, and allocating time to it
  • Convincing the sales team to let me onboard calls with new users
  • Doing research right after a sales pitch full of positives, which made it hard for users to feel comfortable sharing critique
  • Finding users to talk to

What could have been better

  • Time allocation across parts of the project
  • Identifying other problematic steps in the pipeline-creation funnel that were low-hanging fruit with high impact

Learnings

  • Stakeholder management
  • Drawing insights with Google Sheets and Metabase
  • Fullstory as an insight tool
  • Building a very healthy relationship with the team
  • Experiencing the product yourself gives better insight than looking at prototypes or Figma files
